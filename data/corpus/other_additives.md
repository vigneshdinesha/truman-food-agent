# Emulsifiers, Flavor Enhancers, Fats & Whole Foods

## Monosodium Glutamate (MSG, E621)
A flavor enhancer (the sodium salt of glutamic acid, an amino acid found naturally in
tomatoes, cheese, and mushrooms). FDA GRAS. Decades of controlled studies have NOT
confirmed the "Chinese restaurant syndrome" reactions once attributed to MSG; the FDA
and major reviews consider it safe for the general population, though a small number
of people report short-lived symptoms to large doses taken without food. A good example
of an ingredient with a scary reputation but reassuring evidence. Source: FDA GRAS
statement on MSG; FASEB review.

## Soy Lecithin (E322)
An emulsifier derived from soybean oil processing, used to blend fats and water
(chocolate, spreads, baked goods). FDA GRAS; very low concern. Present in trace amounts;
soy-allergic individuals are generally not affected by highly refined lecithin but may
choose to avoid it. Essentially a benign functional additive. Source: FDA GRAS.

## Carrageenan (E407)
A thickener/stabilizer extracted from red seaweed, used in dairy and plant milks. FDA-
permitted for food-grade carrageenan. Some animal studies and lab work suggested
gut inflammation, and it's a frequent target on "gut health" lists, but human evidence
is limited and regulators still permit it. A fair "worth knowing" for people with
digestive sensitivity. Source: FDA; EFSA re-evaluation of carrageenan.

## Palm Oil
A vegetable oil, not a synthetic additive — used for texture and shelf stability. The
health note is nutritional: it's high in saturated fat, so it counts toward
saturated-fat limits. (There are also sustainability/environmental concerns separate
from personal health.) Not a "chemical" red flag; treat like other saturated fats —
fine in moderation. Source: AHA guidance on saturated fat.

## Xanthan Gum (E415)
A fermentation-derived thickener, widely used and well tolerated. FDA GRAS; very low
concern at food levels (large amounts can have a laxative effect). Common in
gluten-free baking. Source: FDA GRAS.

## Whole, Single-Ingredient Foods (baseline)
Foods that are a single whole ingredient — plain oats, brown rice, raw nuts, fresh
produce, plain meat, eggs — contain no additives and are the baseline "clean" case.
When an ingredient list is short and made of recognizable whole foods, the correct
verdict is usually "clean," and Truman should not manufacture concern. The presence of
many unpronounceable additives is what warrants closer attention — not the mere fact
that a label exists. Source: dietary-pattern guidance (whole-food emphasis), WHO/AHA.
