# Sugar Alcohols (Polyols) & Other Sweeteners

## Erythritol (E968)
A near-zero-calorie sugar alcohol popular in "keto" and sugar-free products; occurs
naturally in small amounts in fruit. Generally well tolerated (less GI upset than other
polyols). Note: a 2023 observational study linked **high blood levels of erythritol to
increased cardiovascular events** (heart attack, stroke) — this is association, not
proven cause, and regulators still consider it safe, but it moved erythritol from
"harmless" to "worth-knowing" pending more research. Source: FDA GRAS; Nature Medicine
2023 (Witkowski et al.).

## Xylitol (E967)
A sugar alcohol with dental benefits (used in gum), roughly as sweet as sugar with
~40% fewer calories. Safe for humans in moderation; **large amounts cause bloating and
a laxative effect**. Important: xylitol is **highly toxic to dogs** even in small
amounts — a genuine safety note for pet owners. Source: FDA; ASPCA/veterinary guidance.

## Sorbitol (E420) & Maltitol (E965)
Sugar alcohols used in sugar-free candy and gum. Lower-calorie and don't spike blood
sugar as much as sugar, but they are **poorly absorbed and cause gas, bloating, and a
laxative effect in larger amounts** — products often carry an "excess consumption may
have a laxative effect" warning. Maltitol also raises blood sugar more than most
polyols. Verdict: worth-knowing (digestive), not a toxicity concern. Source: EFSA
opinions on polyols.

## Monk Fruit Extract (Luo Han Guo)
A non-caloric sweetener from monk fruit, GRAS in the US. Considered a lower-concern
"natural" high-intensity sweetener, similar to purified stevia. No established safety
flags at normal intakes. Source: FDA GRAS notices.

## Agave Syrup
A "natural" liquid sweetener marketed as low-glycemic, but it is **very high in
fructose** (often higher than HFCS). Metabolically it's still an added sugar and should
be limited like any other. Verdict: treat as added sugar; the "natural/healthy" halo is
misleading. Source: WHO/AHA added-sugar guidance.

## Invert Sugar / Dextrose / Cane Sugar / Fruit-Juice Concentrate
Different names that all function as **added sugar**. A common label tactic is to split
sugar across several names so none appears first in the ingredient list. Truman should
treat these collectively as added sugar and judge by total amount, not the name.
Source: WHO guideline on sugars; FDA added-sugars labeling.
