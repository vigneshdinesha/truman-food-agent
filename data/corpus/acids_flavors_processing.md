# Acids, Flavors, Fortification & Processing Aids

## Citric Acid (E330)
An acidulant and preservative giving tartness and stability to drinks, candy, and canned
foods. Naturally in citrus, but the commercial form is mostly produced by fermentation
of the mold *Aspergillus niger*. FDA GRAS and considered very low concern; a small
number of people report sensitivity to the manufactured form. Verdict: clean/low
concern. Source: FDA GRAS.

## Phosphoric Acid (E338)
The acid that gives colas their sharp bite. FDA GRAS. The concern is not toxicity but
that high cola intake (phosphoric acid + sugar) is associated in some studies with lower
bone mineral density and dental erosion. Verdict: worth-knowing for heavy cola drinkers.
Source: FDA; observational bone-density studies.

## Lactic Acid (E270) & Malic Acid (E296)
Common food acids for tartness and preservation (lactic acid also from fermentation).
Both FDA GRAS and low concern. Source: FDA GRAS.

## "Natural Flavors" and "Artificial Flavors"
Umbrella terms for proprietary flavor blends. **"Natural" means the flavor molecules
originate from a plant/animal source; "artificial" means they're synthesized** — but
chemically they can be identical, and "natural" is not automatically healthier or
cleaner. Both are FDA-regulated and generally safe; the real downside is opacity
(companies aren't required to disclose the specific components, which can hide allergens
for very sensitive people). Verdict: usually low concern, but "natural flavor" is a
marketing halo, not a health guarantee. Source: FDA 21 CFR 101.22.

## Disodium Inosinate (E631) & Disodium Guanylate (E627)
Flavor enhancers almost always paired with MSG to boost savory (umami) taste — if you
see them, the product is engineered for intense flavor. FDA GRAS and safe for the
general population; like MSG, they carry an outdated scary reputation without strong
evidence of harm. Source: FDA GRAS.

## Yeast Extract / Hydrolyzed Vegetable Protein
Savory flavor ingredients that **naturally contain glutamate** — effectively a way to
add MSG-like flavor while the label can avoid saying "MSG." Not a safety concern for the
general population; relevant only to people specifically avoiding free glutamate.
Verdict: low concern; a labeling-transparency note. Source: FDA guidance on glutamate.

## Enriched Flour, Folic Acid & Fortification (Niacin, Iron, Thiamine, Riboflavin)
"Enriched" flour has B-vitamins and iron added back after milling; **folic-acid
fortification of grains is a public-health success that sharply reduced neural-tube birth
defects**. These are beneficial additions, not red flags. The "refined/enriched" note is
about the flour being refined (less fiber), not about the vitamins being harmful.
Verdict: clean/beneficial. Source: FDA fortification program; CDC folic-acid data.

## EDTA (Calcium Disodium EDTA, E385)
A chelating agent that binds trace metals to prevent color/flavor loss in dressings and
canned goods. FDA-permitted at low levels and considered safe at those levels; very high
doses can interfere with mineral absorption, but dietary exposure is far below that.
Verdict: low concern. Source: FDA; EFSA re-evaluation.

## Sodium/Calcium Propionate (E281/E282)
Mold-inhibiting preservatives in bread and baked goods. FDA GRAS and among the
lower-concern preservatives; a minority of people report sensitivity. Verdict: low
concern. Source: FDA GRAS.

## Caffeine
A natural stimulant, added to sodas and energy drinks. Safe for most adults up to
~400 mg/day; concerns are dose-dependent (sleep disruption, anxiety, heart-rate) and
higher for children, pregnant people, and the caffeine-sensitive. Verdict: worth-knowing
by dose. Source: FDA caffeine guidance.
